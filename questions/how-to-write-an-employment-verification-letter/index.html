How to Write an Employment Verification Letter

An employment verification letter is an official document written by an employer to confirm a person's current job status, hire date, job title, and salary. This document helps landlords, lenders, and mortgage processors verify your income and employment history during an application review.

Published 2 November 2026 · ApprovalPrep

What is an employment verification letter?

An employment verification letter is a formal document requested by landlords, mortgage lenders, and loan officers to confirm your current work status. When you apply for an apartment, a home loan, or credit, reviewers need proof that you earn a steady income. This letter provides that proof directly from the source. It typically comes from your employer's human resources department or your direct manager. The letter states your official job title, your hire date, your current employment status, and your earnings. It acts as a bridge of trust between your stated financial information and the decision-maker evaluating your file.

Reviewers rely on this document because it is harder to forge than standard bank statements or pay stubs. It uses company letterhead and includes contact information so the reviewer can call or email the employer to confirm the details. If you are self-employed or run your own business, the verification process differs, but for traditional employees, this letter remains a standard requirement. Keeping your paperwork clear and accurate helps prevent delays in your application review.

What details must the letter include?

A standard employment verification letter needs specific information to satisfy lenders and landlords. First, it should be printed on official company letterhead to show authenticity. It must include the date the letter is written, the employer's name, the company address, and a contact phone number or email address. The body of the letter needs to state your full legal name, your exact job title, and whether you work full-time, part-time, or on a seasonal basis. It must also list your official hire date so the reviewer can see your length of service with the company.

Next, the letter should state your current compensation details. This usually includes your salary or hourly wage, along with your average hours worked per week. If you earn bonuses or commissions, the employer may include those figures separately or as part of your total annual earnings. Finally, the letter must end with a signature from an authorized representative, such as a human resources manager, along with their printed name and official title. Missing any of these key elements can cause the recipient to reject the document and ask for a replacement.

Who should write and sign the letter?

The employer always writes and signs the employment verification letter. You should never write this letter yourself and sign your employer's name, as doing so constitutes falsification of documents and can lead to application denial or legal trouble. Instead, you submit a formal request to your human resources department, personnel office, or business owner. Many mid-size and large companies have a standard template they use for all employee verification requests. They fill in your specific dates and salary information, print it on company letterhead, and sign it.

If you work for a small business without a dedicated human resources department, your manager or business owner can write the letter. They must use official company stationery or a company-branded digital letterhead. The person who signs the document must have the authority to speak on behalf of the business regarding personnel records. When you request the letter, give your employer plenty of time, as busy human resources departments may take several business days to process and complete verification paperwork.

How to handle special employment situations

Employment situations vary, and standard templates may not fit every applicant. If you recently changed jobs, you may need verification letters from both your current employer and your previous employer to prove a continuous work history. Lenders and landlords often look for a stable two-year employment history, especially when evaluating mortgage applications. If you hold multiple jobs, you will need a separate verification letter from each employer to accurately represent your total monthly income.

If you are self-employed, an employment verification letter from a traditional employer does not apply to you. Instead, you must provide alternative documentation to prove your income. This typically includes profit and loss statements, business bank account statements, and recent tax returns. Independent contractors and small business owners often work with accountants to prepare these alternative verification packets. Always check with your specific lender or landlord to find out what alternative documents they accept if you do not have a traditional employer.

Frequently asked questions

Can I write my own employment verification letter if my boss is busy?

No, you must never write your own employment verification letter or sign your employer's name. Doing so falsifies records and leads to immediate application rejection. Always request that your human resources department or direct manager draft and sign the document on company letterhead.

What if my company refuses to provide a salary amount in the letter?

Some company policies restrict human resources departments from disclosing exact salary figures in writing. If your employer has this restriction, you can provide alternative income proof such as recent pay stubs, W-2 forms, or direct bank statements showing your regular payroll deposits.

Does the employment verification letter need to be notarized by a public notary?

No, employment verification letters rarely require notarization. Lenders and landlords generally accept letters printed on official company letterhead with a verifiable signature and contact phone number. Check your specific application instructions to see if your reviewer requires any special certification or notarization steps.

How long is an employment verification letter valid after it is signed?

Most lenders and landlords consider an employment verification letter valid for thirty to sixty days from the date of signature. Because job status and income can change quickly, reviewers prefer fresh documents. If your application process takes longer than expected, you may need to request a newly dated letter.
